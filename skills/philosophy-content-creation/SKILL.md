# Philosophy Content Creation

Create philosophical content that meets readers where they are. Lead with lived experience, not definitions. Let the name emerge from the story.

## Always-On Rules

1. **Show, don't define.** Never lead with a term and then explain it. Lead with the experience the reader already has, then reveal the name. The reader should feel the truth in their body before they get the vocabulary for it.

2. **Three-tier system.** Structure content in three difficulty levels:
   - **Valley (v0.3):** Zero jargon. Pure narrative, metaphor, story. For the curious newcomer who will bounce if hit with terminology.
   - **Ridge (v0.6):** Names and frameworks emerge. For readers who felt something in the Valley and want to go deeper.
   - **Peak (v0.9):** Full formal architecture. For practitioners ready to measure, diagnose, and engineer.

3. **Observations are not facts.** When making analytical connections across domains (geometry, mysticism, science), frame them as observations or interpretations — not established facts. Use language like "this suggests" or "one way to read this" rather than "this is." User correction: stating "the arbelos encodes consciousness" as fact was overreach; it should have been labeled as metaphor, interpretation, or untested hypothesis.

4. **Ask before gathering.** When the user has existing work (drawings, notes, prior research), ask about their original intuition before gathering external research. Their insight is the signal; external data is context.

5. **Don't overstate cultural markers.** Avoid overstating cultural or linguistic markers (e.g., "Jamaicanized") when the user has not employed them. If the text uses standard English without patois, don't frame it as culturally inflected unless the user confirms that framing.

## Procedure

### Step 1: Understand the Core Concept
- What is the philosophical idea?
- What lived experience demonstrates it?
- What is the reader's current state when they encounter this?

### Step 2: Find the Entry Point
- What does the reader already know or feel?
- What story, metaphor, or image will make them recognize the truth?
- What is the "quiet part" they haven't said out loud?

### Step 3: Draft the Narrative
- Lead with the experience, not the definition
- Use concrete imagery over abstract concepts
- Let the name emerge naturally (if at all)
- Keep the tone like a wise friend, not a textbook

### Step 4: Check for Jargon Creep
- Remove any term a layperson wouldn't know
- Replace definitions with demonstrations
- Ensure the Valley version has ZERO formal terminology

## Pitfalls

- **Don't overstate connections.** When linking geometry to mysticism to science, frame as observation not fact. User correction: stating "the arbelos is a visual encoding of how consciousness organizes itself" as an unqualified fact exceeded the evidence. It must be labeled metaphor, interpretation, or untesting hypothesis.
- **Don't dump research.** When the user has drawings or notes, ask what they saw before gathering external papers. Their intuition is the primary source.
- **Don't define prematurely.** The name should be the reward for reading, not the entry fee.
- **Don't overstate cultural markers.** If the user's text uses standard English without patois, don't frame it as "Jamaicanized" or culturally inflected unless they confirm that framing. - **Preserve the user's uncertainty.** If the user writes "Gene glue?" with a question mark, keep the question mark. Don't transcribe it as settled text.
